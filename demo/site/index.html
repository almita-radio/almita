What is simulated, and how

Sky: the HI4PI 21 cm survey (HI4PI Collaboration 2016, A&A 594, A116), tile CAR_E01, through a Gaussian beam. The tile only covers l −1…21°, b −11…+11°, so a wide beam is truncated at its edges (the map says how much of the beam falls inside). Antenna: TA = η · TB, η = 0.5 (assumed). Receiver: Tsys = 120 K (ALMITA's Y-factor of 12 Nov 2026), a band shape that rolls off beyond ±1 MHz, a spike at the LO. Noise: the radiometer equation, σ = 1/√(Δν·t) per 6.1 kHz channel. Velocity: LSRK → topocentric for 6 Dec 2026, 00:00 Chile, from Santiago.

Frequency switching (what ALMITA really does): half the time the LO sits δ below the line, half δ above. S = (A − B)/B cancels the band shape; the line appears twice, positive and negative, and folding the two copies gives the spectrum. Narrow throw (±300 kHz) leaves a wider folded band but puts the negative "ghost" of a wide line inside it. A linear baseline is fitted outside the line window a rotation model expects; whatever ripple is left stays in the spectrum, as it does on real nights. Near l = 0° the expected line fills most of the folded band, so the baseline is fitted on one side only and the spectrum can sit tilted below the input: that is the instrument, not the sky.

Why two HI4PI curves: a 20° beam averages so much sky that HI4PI looks almost the same in every direction of this region. A high correlation with the "true" spectrum therefore proves you see hydrogen with the right velocity, not that you pointed at that exact spot, unless it also beats the spectrum from 8° away. ALMITA's own 2026-11-25 audit learned that the hard way. Try the 5° beam.
